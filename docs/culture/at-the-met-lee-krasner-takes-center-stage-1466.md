At the Met, Lee Krasner takes center stage

Art Review

*KRASNER AND POLLOCK: PAST CONTINUOUS*

At The Metropolitan Museum of Art, 1000 Fifth Ave., New York, N.Y. Through Jan. 31. 212-535-7710, metmuseum.org

NEW YORK — “Is this a painting?’’ Jackson Pollock asked his wife, Lee Krasner, in 1945, showing her a piece he’d made on the floor in their Long Island home with drips and splatters and not a single brush stroke. The experiment had prompted not revelation but self-doubt. He needed her to know for sure.

It’s not as famous a moment as it should be. There’s a reason for that. As legend had it, Pollock was the intuitive genius and Krasner the silent, stoic, long-suffering support structure that kept his brilliance from shambling into full-blown chaos.

But legends are simple things, and often not quite right because of it. The fractious, tragic relationship between Krasner and Pollock has always been one of those, until pretty much right now. “Krasner and Pollock: Past Continuous,’’ the riveting, revelatory, and beautiful new exhibition at the Metropolitan Museum of Art, changes all that. It explores the deeper dynamics of their romantic and creative alliance, unapologetically blowing apart Pollock’s claim to singular, naturalistic brilliance. The fact that Krasner takes top billing here is heavy with intent. Pollock, the kingpin of the Abstract Expressionist movement, needed — relied on — her help, her eye. With so much of her work here, she’s finally at center stage, nose-to-nose with her famous husband. In many ways that count, this is *her* show, long overdue, and recognition at last of her underacknowledged genius.

There’s no getting away from the tumult of their shared life, though the exhibition’s deft handling of it shows little interest in their drama. A brief passage midway through the show is the only time either Pollock or Krasner are pictured at all, in just four black-and-white photos at the home they shared in Springs on Long Island. It’s something to resist the temptation of tragic theater that their marriage offers. Their relationship toggled between symbiotic and, in its darkest moments, parasitic, as the burden of Pollock’s sudden fame fractured their marriage and sent him careening into the alcoholism that would take his life.

Instead, what we have is painting — so much painting, more than 100 works, many of them overwhelmingly powerful, all of them carefully chosen to give clarity to a partnership that for years has felt one-sided. The push and pull between Krasner and Pollock made him who he was. To him, her opinion mattered most, guiding, coaxing, and reassuring him to the apex of the art world. The weight of his influence left a heavy mark on her too, often painfully, and the exhibition pulls few punches; her grief — and fury — at his death in a drunken car accident with his mistress in 1956 takes explosive form in a bleak and ferocious suite of paintings made after the crash, the pictorial embodiment of a primal scream.

 ![](../wp-content/uploads/sites/953/2026/11/import/image_3_11012026_7ca76882-d3bb-11f1-b629-0793d06fd25c-2cdc5b901838910ef1a1b478208ffd9b.jpg) 

Where the exhibition begins matters: with four works by Krasner, made within a year or two of Pollock’s death. They are rough-textured collages composed of strips of aborted paintings — some of them his — that coalesce in an exuberant fury. The biggest, “Burning Candles,’’ from 1955, is a creative eruption. Look closely and you’ll see the swipes and smears of the paintings and drawings — some hers, some his — that had to die for “Burning Candles’’ to live. It feels like a declaration, an emergence, a breaking away. It’s thrilling.

It’s also a bold move: When Pollock’s in your exhibition title, people expect *Pollock —* up front, center stage — because that’s how it’s always been. Inside, his greatest hits await — so many of them — vast and kinetic and engulfing in their fractious energy. Withholding Pollock up front would be a statement; supplanting him says something else entirely. Pay attention, the curators are telling you. You know *him*. This is something new.

Exposition here is carefully chosen, positioning Krasner independently in the burgeoning avant garde of American Modernism. A tightly-packed gallery of early works, hers and his, situates her at the Hans Hofmann School of Art, the laboratory of Abstract Expressionism, in the late 1930s. Her small works hung nearby ally her with the Cubist ethos he had brought back with him from Paris, where he traveled in the same circles as Pablo Picasso, Henri Matisse, and Georges Braque. Krasner’s pieces, charcoal drawings and oil on paper, are magnetic, fluent and effortless. They strike a hard contrast with the early Pollock works hanging on an adjacent wall, turgid and dour. With its jagged bodies and visceral forms, you can see clearly that Pollock — less schooled, more self-critical — worked harder to achieve less.

The show, briefly, includes a chrysalis moment: a small gallery devoted to “American and French Painting,’’ the 1942 landmark exhibition curated by John Graham that, significantly, put the rising American avant garde — Willem de Kooning, Stuart Davis — on the same walls as its European kin like Matisse, Picasso, and Modigliani. More significantly, the show brought Krasner and Pollock together for the first time; they met as Graham planned the show, and were dating by the time it opened.

From there, it’s a whirlwind: Pollock’s stunning rise, paralleled by Krasner’s fade. Not long after they met, Pollock’s heavy hand was growing looser and more expressive, and the art world had noticed. “Stenographic Figure,’’ 1942, with its bright colors and exuberantly fractured figures, owes a debt to Krasner introducing him to Europeans like Matisse and Piet Mondrian. His work would only grow from there. In the same space, you can see him achieve liftoff as his works get bigger, more distinct and assured: “The Moon Woman,’’ 1942, towering 7 feet high, a Picasso-esque figure in fractured profile in a fog of crimson, and “Pashiphae,’’ 1943, with its symphonic, layered chaos of glyphs and colors.

Krasner, meanwhile, was sinking. She suffered three years of profound creative block right after they met and retreated ever further into his growing shadow. By the time they moved to Springs in 1945, he was on his way to becoming “the greatest American painter alive,’’ as Life magazine suggested in 1949. She was en route to becoming his agent, promoter, nursemaid, and widow. That’s often how this story ends — with Pollock’s burgeoning fame hurtling toward the drunken car accident that claimed his life. It’s also where “Krasner and Pollock’’ pivots into deeper reality that explodes that simple myth.

In Springs, Pollock was exploring the rhythm and verve that would make his reputation — action painting, some called it, as he dripped and splattered fluid commercial paints across canvases he laid on the barn floor. With this new direction, he transformed painting from a process that produced an object into, as the critic Harold Rosenberg called it, “not a picture but an event.’’

From here, things move quickly: to an overpowering array of Pollock’s enormous, iconic, absurdly famous works from the 1940s and ’50s, huge canvases bisecting a massive room flooded with diffuse natural light. It’s a stroke of exhibition design genius; the work engulfs in its naturalistic, dynamic splendor. It’s one of the most important moments in American cultural history come vividly to life.

 ![](../wp-content/uploads/sites/953/2026/11/import/image_4_11012026_7ca76882-d3bb-11f1-b629-0793d06fd25c-6f53d2b54224c380712cc2cc1c84d0ba.jpg) 

But for the first time, they share the stage with Krasner’s own masterworks: eight towering collage pieces made for a 1955 exhibition at the Stable Gallery. They occupy the same vast space, right next to each other, and the equilibrium is striking. Pollock’s most famous work, owned by the likes of MoMA and the Met, in perfect balance with Krasner’s rarely-seen pieces, some still in private collections and (assumably) hanging in people’s homes. Here we see firsthand the power of the myth. Krasner’s work is startlingly original, crafted from strips of canvas and burlap, composed and painted with such an intuitive sense of color and proportion that I don’t hesitate to call them masterworks. They are the exhibition’s greatest revelation, and its gift to art history. I can’t name just one, but if I must: The quiet majesty of “Blue Level,’’ the background stained cobalt and dark lavender while strips of burlap hover on its surface, is lodged permanently in my mind.

What follows is what can only be called a descent: Pollock, overwhelmed by expectation and fame, hunting for a next act he never truly found; and Krasner, in the immediate aftermath of his death, painting huge canvases, soft and pale, in soothing tones of green and pink.

“Earth Green,’’ painted less than a year after his death, feels less like mourning than liberation, loose and free. Pollock was not easy; he soothed his struggles with drinking and womanizing. His death brought grief, but also relief.

It felt like the moment the show could end. In another — or every other — version of this story, it might. But Krasner would live another 30 years, working until her death in 1984. And so, the show — surprisingly, brilliantly — goes on.

From this big, dark gallery of aftermath, Krasner’s “Combat,’’ 1965, a spectacular, fuchsia riot on canvas, pulls you into a winding pathway and back toward the light.

It’s a remarkable feat of exhibition design — a false ending, and a new story begins. Follow the maze through the twists and turns of Krasner’s long career, full of experiment and innovation and liberating work, and watch as “Krasner and Pollock’’ becomes just Krasner, a solo mini-retrospective that she owns by herself. “Kufic,’’ 1965, sparse and almost monochrome, captivates with its sparse mustard swipes on an earthy, ochre ground; “Palingenensis,’’ 1971, an orderly choreography of pink and green forms, takes as its title the Greek word for “rebirth.’’

Through this passageway, you double back to emerge back at the very beginning, where “Burning Candles’’ first greeted you at the outset. And so the circle closes. We start and end with Krasner, finally, it should be.
