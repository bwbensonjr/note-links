# How Troy Jackson toppled Susan Collins in Maine Senate race

PORTLAND, Maine — One symbol for how unlikely [Troy Jackson’s victory over Susan Collins](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/11/04/nation/troy-jackson-beats-susan-collins-maine-senate/ "https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/11/04/nation/troy-jackson-beats-susan-collins-maine-senate/") once seemed is the very room where he celebrated his triumph on Tuesday night.

Just months ago, the Holiday Inn ballroom in downtown Portland [hosted a massive rally for Graham Platner](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/04/18/nation/elizabeth-warren-graham-platner-maine/ "https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/04/18/nation/elizabeth-warren-graham-platner-maine/"), the candidate Democrats were initially convinced would be the one to finally oust Collins.When Platner dropped out in disgrace, many Democrats lost hope, convinced they could not beat the formidable Republican on a tight timeline, in the wreckage of scandal.

Jackson nevertheless took Platner’s place with the daunting 100-day assignment to pry a Senate seat from the hands of the Republican who has most bedeviled Democrats, both in Maine and nationally, with her political resilience. Throughout his short campaign, Jackson was doubted, disrespected, and counted out.

But early Wednesday morning, in that same ballroom, he was exalted. Supporters chanted his name as he took the stage and joined him in singing the labor movement anthem, “Solidarity Forever.”

The demise of Collins’s 30-year Senate career was an unbelievable sight for many in this state she has long dominated — literally. The same polls that showed her trailing Jackson also showed hisown supporters saying they believed Collins would win anyway.

That’s exactly what the Republican did six years ago, instilling in her critics a fatalism that she could never be defeated no matter how hard they tried.

This time, Collins’s time-honored strategy — appealing to independents with practiced centrism and a [parochial focus on the state](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/14/nation/susan-collins-appropriations-maine-troy-jackson/ "https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/10/14/nation/susan-collins-appropriations-maine-troy-jackson/") — ran out of room as anti-Trump backlash fueled an urgency for change.

“I don’t think her defeat is a story, in my view, about, ‘How did Troy Jackson manage to best a veteran politician?’” said Nirav Shah, a Maine public health official who ran against Jackson both for governor and then in the short primary to replace Platner. In Maine, Shah believes, “Trumpism found its logical end, with people saying enough is enough.”

Nevertheless, Shah argued, “Troy and his team deserve every ounce of credit. They should savor this victory.”

Democrats here said it took a combination of several factors — the strength of Jackson’s campaign and the [unity of the labor movement](https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/09/09/nation/troy-jackson-susan-collins-maine/ "https://www.bostonglobe.com/2026/09/09/nation/troy-jackson-susan-collins-maine/"); the national rejection of Trump and his allies; the weight of unsustainable fuel costs and tariffs; and a fierce and deeply personal opposition to ICE tactics witnessed in Maine — to topple Collins.

Given Democratic gains on Tuesday in places like Kansas and South Carolina, victory in Maine might have seemed inevitable in retrospect. After the result became clear early Wednesday morning, Collins’s campaign manager told The Wall Street Journal, “I don’t know what we could have done.”

Democrats, who’ve spent years attacking Collins for not being publicly available, scoffed at such spin. In no other state, arguably, did Democrats face such a daunting structural disadvantage as in Maine in the wake of the Platner saga. Jackson, a former logger whose plainspoken style belies a political sense sharpened in state government, had to work hard just to put himself in position to exploit a horrible environment for Republicans.

In just over 100 days, Jackson stood up a campaign apparatus to barnstorm the state, rapidly raised millions of dollars, and perfected a pitch that would retain the progressive energy Platner sparked while preserving his appeal to independents who long backed Collins.

According to Jackson’s campaign, he held more than 100 public campaign events around the state, including 15 town halls, conducted 115 interviews with media, and engaged in four debates with Collins. The campaign ultimately raised in excess of $31 million from more than 280,000 contributors and knocked on over 410,000 doors.

Top Democrats tapped in to boost his campaign, perhaps none more so than Senator Elizabeth Warren. As Jackson’s team readied for the first debate with Collins in October, a daunting task for a politician unsteady on the debate stage, Warren quietly traveled to Maine to help him prepare, according to a senior source on the Jackson campaign.

The two had previously hit it off at a fund-raiser. Warren came ready with detailed feedback after listening to an hourlong recording of Jackson’s first mock debate. She got evenmore specific in person: noticing his penchant for fidgeting, she advised him to wiggle his toes so he’d stop squirming above the podium.

It was, the Jackson source said, the pep talk the candidate needed.

Originally from the remote north woods hamlet of Allagash, Jackson spent much of the 2026 election cycle running an unsuccessful campaign for governor.

After Platner’s exit, at a hastily arranged party convention to replace him, Jackson quickly consolidated support, leaning heavily on his allies in the labor movement.It was an early indication of the organizing muscle he would bring to the general election.

“For a while, it was just . . . crazy” after Platner dropped out, said Greg Marley, chairman of the Knox County Democrats. “There’s that time that was needed for people to get behind Jackson, because they didn’t see him in that role. He is not the dynamic orator that Graham Platner is.”

At first, Jackson himself remarked often about the unusual circumstances of his Senate run. Effectively, he was running for an office that was not his first choice, as the candidate who was not voters’ first choice.

Marley admitted that early on, “I was one of those doubters” of Jackson. “I was not certain he had the chops. But the more I saw him, in large settings, in small settings, the more comfortable I got that he has the chops.”

As the race progressed, confidence in Jackson faltered in some corners. Democrats had watched strong candidates fall to Collins before.

“We were very conscious of just how difficult this mountain would be to climb,” said Glenn Cummings, a former speaker of the Maine House of Representatives.

Cummings praised Jackson for bringing the campaign together quickly, and said he believed the negative advertising from Collins and her allies — in particular, Maine television personality Bill Green, who attacked Jackson’s capabilities and intelligence when he cracked that “we’re not buying a cord of wood here” — had worked against the Republican’s candidacy in the end.

“It was a vital mistake when he started to disparage loggers,” Cummings said.

For Maine Democrats, Wednesday was an unseasonably good day in more ways than one. In Portland the sun was shining, the temperature was nearing 70 degrees, and Collins had fallen. Also, Hannah Pingree was elected governor, retaining the post for Democrats, while the party [won a supermajority in the state Senate](https://www.facebook.com/theDLCC/posts/breaking-democrats-just-won-a-supermajority-in-the-maine-senate-and-expanded-the/1574403668049986/) and expanded its advantage in the state House.

“I mean . . .” said Ed Suslovic, grinning, as he gestured behind him to the glorious blue sky hovering over Casco Bay. Suslovic, a former state legislator and Portland city councilor, had gathered with dozens of other Pingree supporters to celebrate the Democratic sweep at Fort Allen Park along the water in Portland, where the mood was buoyant.

Suslovic said that until recently, he “did not dare to hope” that Jackson could topple Collins.

Ultimately, though, “people were able to rally around Troy,” Suslovic said. “His commitment to standing up for the working people of Maine is genuine.”

For decades, Collins has left Maine Democrats feeling like Captain Ahab. On Tuesday, as a blue wave broke over the country, they claimed their white whale.

“I feel exultation,” said Molly Schen, 66, a Democratic volunteer who lives in South Portland.

Darcy Halvorsen, a member of Maine’s Democratic State Committee, said she is a “pessimist by nature.” Even late polling showing Jackson in the lead didn’t persuade her, she said, because she’d seen Collins defy the odds before.

But by the early hours of Wednesday morning, Halvorsen was one of the Maine Democrats celebrating. She even ran into Jackson in the hallway of the hotel where the afterparty was being held.

After knowing him 20 years, Halvorsen joked to Jackson that she was going to have trouble calling him “senator.”

Jackson reassured her, she said: “ ‘You can always call me Troy.’ ”
