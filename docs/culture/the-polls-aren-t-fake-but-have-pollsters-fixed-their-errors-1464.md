# The polls aren’t fake. But have pollsters fixed their errors?

![](../wp-content/uploads/sites/953/2026/11/import/image_main_11012026_0906d044-d39c-11f1-b629-0793d06fd25c-d1dd13f6d9c3777d54f678a54e2f4561-768x445.jpg)

The polls aren’t fake. But have pollsters fixed their errors?
=============================================================

No one fully trusts them, yet no one can turn away. But whether or not the polls are right Tuesday, pollsters have adjusted for famous misses in 2016 and 2024

By Brian Schaffner
|
November 1st, 2026, 3:42 am

*Brian Schaffner is a political science professor at Tufts University and directs the Cooperative Election Study, the nation’s largest academic poll.*

It’s days before the 2026 midterms, and the only thing Democrats and Republicans can agree on is that the polls might be wrong.

No lead feels safe for skittish Democrats burned by polling errors in 2016 and 2024. President Trump and his FCC chairman, meanwhile, have taken to calling preelection polls “fake’’and “suppressive.’’ (They’ve received support from revelations that Wedgewood Polls, whose surveys were included in many prominent media aggregations, fabricated its data.) Republicans are banking on the notion that polls will be off once again.

Despite the anxieties and fears on both sides, we as a nation have as much of an appetite as ever for polling data. Polls, after all, are the best tool we have for setting expectations about what the election results might look like on Tuesday. The polls may well miss. But if the results are surprising, it won’t be because pollsters have failed to adjust after the mistakes of recent elections.

A week after the 2016 election, as shocked Americans digested just how badly many polls had performed, I appeared on a panel that had been designed as the usual election postmortem. It felt more like a reckoning for a profession forever changed.

There was much angst in the room. But what stood out was the calm presentation from a prominent New England pollster whose final preelection New Hampshire poll indicated that Hillary Clinton would win the state by 11 points. She won by less than half a percentage point. The poll was off by a whopping double digits.

What went wrong? The pollster walked through a series of explanations, but the culprit was clear. The sample of respondents was carefully collected, but the pollster only made adjustments to ensure that it represented New Hampshire voters on the usual three dimensions: age, gender, and region. These tweaks had always worked in the past. The pollster did not believe 2016 would be any different. Of course, it was.

Then the pollster delivered a rueful punchline: If the firm had simply adjusted to make the poll’s sample reflective of the Granite State’s education levels, it would have nailed the result.

Trump’s unexpected victory was a watershed moment for polling. And it arrived amid a collision of political, technological, and cultural changes to which some pollsters were late to respond.

As Americans embraced caller ID to screen calls, ditched landlines, and listened to politicians who told them to distrust pollsters and the media, it became impossible for pollsters to collect a truly random sample. Response rates plummeted to under 5 percent, meaning that for every 100 people a poll tried to contact, fewer than five agreed to talk. A random, representative sample is at the heart of a poll’s credibility. Yet many pollsters carried on as if nothing had changed.

But after 2016, the industry finally recognized that what it would take to produce an accurate poll had been altered forever.

Here’s the technical piece: No matter how a pollster goes about collecting their sample, whether it be phone calls, online solicitations, or text messages, every sample is essentially an odd group of folks who actually want to answer a bunch of questions about politics. Even the highest-quality polls that make the most herculean efforts to reach voters have response rates below 3 percent. Take the September Siena/New York Times poll, which had to place calls to more than 85,000 registered voters in order to get 1,503 people to complete the survey. That’s less than 2 percent.

Unlike the Siena/New York Times poll, most surveys aren’t even conducted by phone anymore. Most pollsters tap pools of people who have agreed to complete online surveys to earn small rewards. This audience is just as small and self-selected as the one that agrees to answer a telephone survey.

That’s a huge shift. Accurate polling is now much less about whomakes it into the sample, and much more about how pollsters statistically *adjust* their samples to make it resemble a random sample. This is typically achieved through an approach called weighting. For example, that 2016 New Hampshire poll had way too many college-educated voters: 61 percent in a state where fewer than 40 percent have a college degree. Weighting the sample would involve making the college-educated respondents count less and those without a college degree count more, in order to have a survey that reflects the state’s population.

Pollsters did not weight their data nearly enough before 2016. That has changed. In 2024, a student and I wanted to get a sense of how pollsters were weighting their polls. We collected the data for all national and swing-state polls fielded during the final few weeks of the campaign. We compared that data to a similar set another student and I had collected in 2016. The shifts were striking. In 2016, only about one out of every four state polls was weighted for education. By 2024, three out of every four were.

The other major pattern we observed is an increase in the number of pollsters who weight their data for either party affiliation or whom people voted for in the past election. Nate Silver sees this practice as akin to cheating. He suggests that in polarized times, weighting by party allows pollsters to play it safe to avoid having their polls be too far off the mark. Some in the polling industry agree. But others, like me, argue that we should do as much as we can to ensure that our samples aren’t underrepresenting Republicans or Trump voters. We know how many people voted for Trump in 2024. Why not use that information to ensure that we have the same proportion in our sample?

Regardless of where one lands on this particular debate, it reflects a simple truth about modern polling: Almost every reputable pollster is using statistical modeling to adjust their samples, but there is no industry standard. This helps explain how a poll can be off and why polls disagree: In many ways, pollsters aren’t just asking questions of voters any more. They are modeling demographics and turnout.

You can see this with two polls released in the Iowa Senate race in early October from two well-regarded pollsters. The Marist University poll showed Democrat Josh Turek ahead by 3 points after the data was weighted for age, gender, income, and race. A poll from CNN/SSRS showed Republican Ashley Hinson up by 2 points. That poll also weighted for age, gender, and race, but not income. Instead, the pollsters weighted for how the respondent voted in 2024.

And all these choices must be made before pollsters get to the task of identifying who will actually vote in the election. This so-called “likely voter’’ modeling is even more complex and opaque than weighting.

It is an important advance for the polling industry to expand how it adjusts its samples and account for ways in which they are not representative. But this also means that it is as difficult as ever to read any one poll alongside another. After all, there are about as many different ways to weight a survey as there are pollsters.

It is often difficult or even impossible for experts to uncover weighting information about a poll, much less to make sense of it. When we collected this data for polls fielded in 2024, 30 percent of the time we were unable to determine how a pollster weighted a survey even after trying to email or message them directly. The best pollsters do make this information public, but it can be challenging to digest even for someone with technical expertise. The pollsters behind that recent Siena/New York Times poll took some 1,500 words to describe their approach, an explanation about as long as this article.

So what’s a voter to do? Polls are everywhere, especially this time of year, and it’s natural to set expectations or gauge the national mood through them. Yet polls are both data science and an art. Each one is a portrait of the electorate, with a set of assumptions. Poll aggregators can help people get a fuller picture of where a race stands. But remember, they average away the important decisions being made by individual pollsters in the hope that those decisions will cancel out. That means they should not fixate on the results from any given poll — and should treat the differences between polls as information about how uncertain a race is, rather than noise to be averaged away.

In some cases, it is better to understand why two polls are showing different patterns rather than simply split the difference between them. Our analysis of the 2024 polls showed that weighting for education and past vote choice tended to make polls more accurate, so you might favor polls that weight for those factors over ones that don’t.

The key thing to keep in mind is that a poll in 2026 is a reasoned estimate from a particular pollster using a particular method. Reasonable pollsters are making those estimates differently, and the differences are now the thing to pay attention to — not as errors in the samples but as the judgment calls built into the adjustments. That pollster surveying New Hampshire learned an important lesson after 2016, and so did the polling industry.

The polls may miss in 2026. But it won’t be because pollsters are doing too little to adjust their estimates. It will be because they’ve made the wrong adjustments.

By Brian Schaffner
