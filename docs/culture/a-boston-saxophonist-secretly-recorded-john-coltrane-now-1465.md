# A Boston saxophonist secretly recorded John Coltrane. Now the tapes are coming out.

![](../wp-content/uploads/sites/953/2026/11/import/image_main_11012026_1a2d4ae8-d3d2-11f1-b629-0793d06fd25c-3ddd031b278ac9fed56a8b20334bb172.jpg)

![](../wp-content/uploads/sites/953/2026/11/import/image_2_11012026_1a2d4ae8-d3d2-11f1-b629-0793d06fd25c-1c0d9172dfd1b56e59ca920c77fa766c.jpg) 

When Joe Lovano joined Woody Herman’s big band in 1976, he discovered that one of his fellow saxophonists had a secret stash of reel-to-reel recordings. “Sometimes on the bus he’d put headphones on me,’’ Lovano recalled.

The bandmate was Frank Tiberi. While the big band was barnstorming, he’d let the other members listen to the hours of live recordings he had secretly made of John Coltrane in the early 1960s. Those closely guarded tapes would acquire mythological status, especially in Greater Boston, where Tiberi is a longtime Easton resident and Berklee College of Music faculty member.

Now, at 97, he’s finally sharing the recordings with the world via the new four-LP box set “John Coltrane: The Tiberi Tapes,’’ which arrives Nov. 6 as part of Coltrane centennial celebrations.

“It’s a very big deal within the music world,’’ said Ashley Kahn, the author who penned the release’s liner notes. As famous as the tapes have become in jazz musician circles, Kahn found that even some of Tiberi’s bandmates had never heard them.

“These are treasures that have long been discussed,’’ he said.

The tapes, made between 1960 and 1964, capture a crucial period in Coltrane’s development. He had recently left Miles Davis’s band and was starting what would, after a few personnel changes, become his classic quartet with pianist McCoy Tyner, drummer Elvin Jones, and bassist Jimmy Garrison. As groundbreaking as his studio recordings were, Coltrane could stretch out and experiment even more when he played the intimate Philadelphia venues where Tiberi had his tape surreptitiously rolling. Coltrane “was practicing, rehearsing, and exploring new ideas in public every night,’’ said Kahn.

Tiberi wasn’t just a fan. He was already an established working musician often found in Philadelphia orchestra pits — “the first person in Philly who could double’’ by playing multiple wood instruments in a band, pointed out George Garzone, Tiberi’s longtime friend, fellow tenor player, and Berklee faculty member. When Coltrane had first emerged in the 1950s, Tiberi admitted he “wasn’t impressed.’’

“He was playing everything symmetrically,’’ Tiberi added in an interview at the Berklee office where he gives private woodwinds lessons every week. “But when I heard him play ‘Giant Steps’ and ‘Count Down’ it was something different.’’

“As a fellow saxophonist, Frank was able to recognize how Coltrane was pushing the performance of improvised music to another level,’’ said Kahn. “He knew that he should capture it so he could slow the tape down, hear what’s going on, and learn from it.’’

Less obvious was how Tiberi could capture what Coltrane was doing.

“I bought a little machine with mylar tape. I hid it in my satchel,’’ recalled Tiberi. The condenser microphone and its wires were hidden in Tiberi’s coat. “One time McCoy, who used to play with me, saw the satchel and said, ‘Oh, you just came from school.’ I had wires up the wazoo!’’

When it came time to change the tape, Tiberi had to reach inside the satchel and do it without looking so the club bouncers wouldn’t notice. He wouldn’t always succeed, and the tapes weren’t always labeled. As a result, the “Tiberi Tapes’’ producers had to fill in some of the discographic details normally found on historic jazz releases, and the audio quality reflects the equipment that was used.

 ![](../wp-content/uploads/sites/953/2026/11/import/image_3_11012026_1a2d4ae8-d3d2-11f1-b629-0793d06fd25c-4f8ac90738a28bfca5594dfcd5674cf8.jpg) 

But those who have heard the tapes say they’re still a revelation.

“The live ‘Giant Steps’ is a masterpiece,’’ said Lovano, who will be featured with Vivo Performing Arts’ all-star Coltrane 100 concert at the Berklee Performance Center Nov. 21. “It’s a contrast to the recording that everyone knows. … His playing was so melodic. It will really open up everyone’s ears.’’

While Coltrane was already playing rarified concert halls and swank Manhattan night clubs, Tiberi said he’d display a different side when playing neighborhood venues.

“At a concert people had paid their money and probably wanted to hear a variety of pieces, not one tune that stretched on for 20 minutes,’’ he said. Among his favorite tapes is an extended version of “Satellite,’’ “where he really builds it up, and by the end of the 18 minutes, the audience is just screaming!’’

The Coltrane on the “Tiberi Tapes’’ “is just really letting go,’’ added Garzone.

After a particularly grueling Jersey Shore summer leading a show band that backed the likes of Don Rickles and the Fifth Dimension, Tiberi told his mother he was going to take it easy on the beach and that he didn’t want any music-related calls, but in 1969, Woody Herman trumpeter Bill Chase tracked him down by chatting with Tiberi’s mother in Italian. Tiberi, then 40 and newly divorced, jumped at the opportunity.

Herman’s band, known as the “Herd,’’ enjoyed a second wind throughout the next decade thanks to college bookings, a collaboration with Frank Sinatra, and multiple Grammys. Its open-minded leader added Coltrane compositions to the book, including Tiberi’s arrangements of “Count Down’’ and “Giant Steps.’’ Shortly before Herman’s death in 1987 Tiberi became the band’s director.

In 1995 Tiberi joined the faculty at Berklee, where he would often use the Coltrane tapes as a teaching tool. Garzone was astonished that one of his idols had moved not just to the Boston area but to Easton, a short drive from Garzone’s Bridgewater home. He got to hear the Coltrane tapes while fishing with Tiberi.

“It was like they put God in my backyard,’’ he said. “I’ve learned so much from him. I still tell all of my kids, ‘You’ve got to study with this guy.’’’

At 97 Tiberi still loves teaching and performing. He played with Lovano at the Rochester International Jazz Festival this summer. Last month, sporting a “Tiberi Tapes’’ T-shirt his son had gifted him, Tiberi joined Garzone’s quartet and fellow Boston tenor titan Jerry Bergonzi for well over two hours of music at the Mad Monkfish in Cambridge. His feature on “Body and Soul’’ showed why Lovano said, “Frank has developed a way of maneuvering through harmonies with a passionate and beautiful voice of his own.’’

In between sets Tiberi mentioned that he’s been getting calls from journalists and historians from all over the world as the “Tiberi Tapes’’ finally, after all these decades of rumor and myth, see the light of day. He said that he is looking forward to hearing the reactions from his fellow Coltrane scholars.

“They waited 60 years for it.’’
